M4 · Numerical Methods for Scattering Problems

Part 0 — Foundations · Status: draft v1 · Companion notebook: M4_numerical_methods.ipynb

Learning goals. After this chapter you should be able to

  • prepare the radial equation for the computer: reduced units, step sizes and matching radii;
  • distinguish local and global errors, and compare the order, cost and stability of the Euler,
    Runge–Kutta and Numerov methods;
  • derive the Numerov method, start it at the origin, and predict its phase error;
  • find bound states by shooting and matching, and label them by counting nodes;
  • integrate coupled equations with closed channels, and recognize and cure the loss of linear
    independence;
  • choose a quadrature rule, and know when the trapezoidal rule is exponentially accurate;
  • propagate wave packets with the split-operator and Crank–Nicolson methods, and build absorbing
    boundaries;
  • resum divergent Born or iteration series with Padé approximants;
  • test a scattering code against information that does not come from the code itself.

Prerequisites: M1 (Riccati and Coulomb
functions) and M2 (Green’s functions and
the Lippmann–Schwinger equation). Chapter 2 uses the methods of this chapter and is a good companion.

Sources: C&H §2.2 (numerical solutions of the radial equation) and §9.5 (solution of the
coupled-channel equations); Thompson (1988) §3.3 (bound states), §5.1 (integration of the radial
equations) and §5.2 (convergence of the iterative method); the FRESCO input manual (QR stabilization
and Padé acceleration); Press et al., Numerical Recipes.

How to read this chapter. Every method here is tested in the notebook on a problem with a known
answer, and the numbers quoted come from those tests. The main thread is M4.1–M4.5: the radial
equation, its bound states and its coupled-channel form. M4.6–M4.8 cover tools that later chapters
need (quadrature, time propagation, series resummation), and M4.9 collects the checks that a
scattering code should pass.

M4.2 From Euler to Runge–Kutta

General-purpose integrators work with first-order systems. Writing y=(u,u′)y = (u, u')y=(u,u′) and
w(r)=k2−l(l+1)/r2−U(r)w(r) = k^2 - l(l+1)/r^2 - U(r)w(r)=k2−l(l+1)/r2−U(r), (M4.1) becomes

y′=f(r,y)=(u′, −w(r) u).(M4.3)y' = f(r, y) = \big(u',\ -w(r)\,u\big) . \tag{M4.3} y′=f(r,y)=(u′, −w(r)u).(M4.3)

The simplest method, forward Euler, follows the tangent:

yn+1=yn+h f(rn,yn).(M4.4)y_{n+1} = y_n + h\,f(r_n, y_n) . \tag{M4.4} yn+1​=yn​+hf(rn​,yn​).(M4.4)

Its local error, made in one step, is O(h2)O(h^2)O(h2). Over a fixed distance LLL the L/hL/hL/h local errors
add up to a global error O(h)O(h)O(h): the method is of first order. In general, a local error
O(hp+1)O(h^{p+1})O(hp+1) gives a global error O(hp)O(h^p)O(hp). The classical fourth-order Runge–Kutta method samples
the slope four times per step:

k1=f(rn,yn),k2=f(rn+h2, yn+h2k1),k3=f(rn+h2, yn+h2k2),k4=f(rn+h, yn+hk3),yn+1=yn+h6 (k1+2k2+2k3+k4),(M4.5)\begin{aligned} &k_1 = f(r_n, y_n),\qquad k_2 = f\big(r_n + \tfrac h2,\ y_n + \tfrac h2k_1\big),\qquad k_3 = f\big(r_n + \tfrac h2,\ y_n + \tfrac h2k_2\big),\qquad k_4 = f(r_n + h,\ y_n + hk_3),\\ &y_{n+1} = y_n + \tfrac h6\,(k_1 + 2k_2 + 2k_3 + k_4) , \end{aligned} \tag{M4.5} ​k1​=f(rn​,yn​),k2​=f(rn​+2h​, yn​+2h​k1​),k3​=f(rn​+2h​, yn​+2h​k2​),k4​=f(rn​+h, yn​+hk3​),yn+1​=yn​+6h​(k1​+2k2​+2k3​+k4​),​(M4.5)

with local error O(h5)O(h^5)O(h5) and global error O(h4)O(h^4)O(h4).

M4.3 The Numerov method

The radial equation has two special features: it is linear, and it has no first-derivative term. The
Numerov method exploits both. Add the Taylor expansions of u(r±h)u(r \pm h)u(r±h):

un+1−2un+un−1=h2un′′+h412un(4)+O(h6),h2un(4)=un+1′′−2un′′+un−1′′+O(h4).(M4.7)u_{n+1} - 2u_n + u_{n-1} = h^2u_n'' + \frac{h^4}{12}u_n^{(4)} + O(h^6),\qquad h^2u_n^{(4)} = u''_{n+1} - 2u''_n + u''_{n-1} + O(h^4) . \tag{M4.7} un+1​−2un​+un−1​=h2un′′​+12h4​un(4)​+O(h6),h2un(4)​=un+1′′​−2un′′​+un−1′′​+O(h4).(M4.7)

Insert the second relation into the first and use u′′=−wuu'' = -wuu′′=−wu. The result is the three-term
recursion of Chapter 2 (eq. 2.18):

(1+h212wn+1)un+1=2(1−5h212wn)un−(1+h212wn−1)un−1.(M4.8)\Big(1 + \frac{h^2}{12}w_{n+1}\Big)u_{n+1} = 2\Big(1 - \frac{5h^2}{12}w_n\Big)u_n - \Big(1 + \frac{h^2}{12}w_{n-1}\Big)u_{n-1} . \tag{M4.8} (1+12h2​wn+1​)un+1​=2(1−125h2​wn​)un​−(1+12h2​wn−1​)un−1​.(M4.8)

Its local error is O(h6)O(h^6)O(h6) and its global error O(h4)O(h^4)O(h4), the same order as RK4. But it costs one

shows Numerov about four times more accurate than RK4 at the same hhh. It reaches 10−610^{-6}10−6 rad with
about 690 evaluations, six times fewer than RK4.

M4.4 Shooting and matching for bound states

A bound state, ε=−κ2<0\varepsilon = -\kappa^2 < 0ε=−κ2<0, must be regular at the origin and decay at infinity.
Outside the potential the general solution is

u(r)=A eκr+B e−κr(for l>0: A κr il(κr)+B κr kl(κr)),(M4.13)u(r) = A\,e^{\kappa r} + B\,e^{-\kappa r}\qquad\big(\text{for } l > 0:\ A\,\kappa r\,i_l(\kappa r) + B\,\kappa r\,k_l(\kappa r)\big), \tag{M4.13} u(r)=Aeκr+Be−κr(for l>0: Aκril​(κr)+Bκrkl​(κr)),(M4.13)

and the regular solution has A=0A = 0A=0 only at the eigenvalues. Two strategies find them.

Shooting. Integrate outward from the origin at a trial energy. Unless the energy is an eigenvalue,
the solution blows up beyond the well, toward +∞+\infty+∞ or −∞-\infty−∞. The sign of the blow-up changes at
every eigenvalue, so bisection on that sign converges to the eigenvalue (Animation M4.1). Shooting is
simple but wasteful: the growing exponential has to be integrated through the forbidden region.

Matching. Integrate outward to a matching radius rmr_mrm​ near the surface, and inward from a large
radius, starting with the decaying solution

uin(r)∝κr kl(κr)(r near rmax⁡).(M4.14)u_{\rm in}(r) \propto \kappa r\,k_l(\kappa r)\qquad(r \text{ near } r_{\max}) . \tag{M4.14} uin​(r)∝κrkl​(κr)(r near rmax​).(M4.14)

Inward integration is stable, because the decaying solution grows toward smaller rrr. The two pieces
belong to one eigenfunction when their logarithmic derivatives agree at rmr_mrm​. The difference of
logarithmic derivatives has poles; a smooth equivalent is the mismatch function

F(E)=sin⁡(θout−θin),θ=arctan⁡u(rm)u′(rm)/q,(M4.15)F(E) = \sin(\theta_{\rm out} - \theta_{\rm in}),\qquad \theta = \arctan\frac{u(r_m)}{u'(r_m)/q} , \tag{M4.15} F(E)=sin(θout​−θin​),θ=arctanu′(rm​)/qu(rm​)​,(M4.15)

with a fixed scale qqq (1 fm⁻¹ in the notebook). It vanishes exactly at the eigenvalues
(Fig. M4.3a), and a secant or Newton search on it converges fast.

Node counting. The oscillation theorem labels the levels: the nnn-th bound state of a partial wave
has n−1n - 1n−1 nodes, and the number of nodes of the regular solution at energy EEE equals the number of
bound states below EEE. Counting nodes therefore brackets every level, and bisection on the count
cannot miss one. Narrow gaps or nearly degenerate levels, which defeat a simple scan of F(E)F(E)F(E), are
no problem. scatlib.numerov.bound_states works this way. It also counts the node that may lie beyond
the grid, from the analytic continuation (M4.13).

M4.5 Coupled equations: the matrix Numerov method

M4.7 Time-dependent problems

The split-operator method of M0 (eq. M0.22) applies e−iVΔt/2e−iTΔte−iVΔt/2e^{-iV\Delta t/2}e^{-iT\Delta t}e^{-iV\Delta t/2}e−iVΔt/2e−iTΔte−iVΔt/2, with the

operations. Both methods conserve the norm exactly.

M4.8 Summing slowly converging series: Padé approximants

The Lippmann–Schwinger equation (M2) can be solved by iteration, which generates the Born series.
For the potential λU\lambda UλU the amplitude T=eiδsin⁡δT = e^{i\delta}\sin\deltaT=eiδsinδ becomes a power series in the
strength λ\lambdaλ:

T(λ)=∑n≥1tnλn,convergent if and only if ∣λ ηi∣<1 for all i,(M4.25)T(\lambda) = \sum_{n\ge1}t_n\lambda^n,\qquad \text{convergent if and only if } |\lambda\,\eta_i| < 1 \text{ for all } i , \tag{M4.25} T(λ)=n≥1∑​tn​λn,convergent if and only if ∣ληi​∣<1 for all i,(M4.25)

where the ηi\eta_iηi​ are the Weinberg eigenvalues, the eigenvalues of the kernel G0UG_0UG0​U. An attractive
potential strong enough to bind has ∣η1∣>1|\eta_1| > 1∣η1​∣>1 at low energy, so its Born series diverges at the
physical strength λ=1\lambda = 1λ=1. The same happens to the iterative solution of coupled-channel
equations when the couplings are strong. Each iteration adds one more order of the coupling (Thompson
1988, §5.2).

Padé approximants. The [L/M][L/M][L/M] Padé approximant is the rational function that agrees with the
series up to the order L+ML + ML+M:

[L/M] (λ)=p0+p1λ+⋯+pLλL1+q1λ+⋯+qMλM,[L/M] (λ)−T(λ)=O(λL+M+1).(M4.26)[L/M]\,(\lambda) = \frac{p_0 + p_1\lambda + \dots + p_L\lambda^L}{1 + q_1\lambda + \dots + q_M\lambda^M},\qquad [L/M]\,(\lambda) - T(\lambda) = O(\lambda^{L+M+1}) . \tag{M4.26} [L/M](λ)=1+q1​λ+⋯+qM​λMp0​+p1​λ+⋯+pL​λL​,[L/M](λ)−T(λ)=O(λL+M+1).(M4.26)

Multiplying out gives linear equations, first for the qjq_jqj​, then for the pkp_kpk​:

∑j=0Mqj ck−j=0(k=L+1,…,L+M, q0=1),pk=∑j=0min⁡(k,M)qj ck−j(k=0,…,L),(M4.27)\sum_{j=0}^{M}q_j\,c_{k-j} = 0\quad(k = L+1, \dots, L+M,\ q_0 = 1),\qquad p_k = \sum_{j=0}^{\min(k,M)}q_j\,c_{k-j}\quad(k = 0, \dots, L) , \tag{M4.27} j=0∑M​qj​ck−j​=0(k=L+1,…,L+M, q0​=1),pk​=j=0∑min(k,M)​qj​ck−j​(k=0,…,L),(M4.27)

where the ckc_kck​ are the series coefficients. The system becomes ill-conditioned when the ckc_kck​ grow
fast; rescaling λ\lambdaλ by the radius of convergence helps. The same diagonal approximants can be
computed from the partial sums sns_nsn​ alone, without solving equations, by Wynn’s ε algorithm:

ε−1(n)=0,ε0(n)=sn,εk+1(n)=εk−1(n+1)+1εk(n+1)−εk(n),(M4.28)\varepsilon^{(n)}_{-1} = 0,\qquad \varepsilon^{(n)}_0 = s_n,\qquad \varepsilon^{(n)}_{k+1} = \varepsilon^{(n+1)}_{k-1} + \frac{1}{\varepsilon^{(n+1)}_k - \varepsilon^{(n)}_k} , \tag{M4.28} ε−1(n)​=0,ε0(n)​=sn​,εk+1(n)​=εk−1(n+1)​+εk(n+1)​−εk(n)​1​,(M4.28)

input variable PADE = 1 uses the ε algorithm, and PADE = 2 the explicit N/DN/DN/D polynomials, to

Why it works. The exact amplitude is

T(λ)=−λk ⟨j^l∣ U (1−λG0U)−1∣j^l⟩,(M4.29)T(\lambda) = -\frac{\lambda}{k}\,\big\langle\hat j_l\big|\,U\,(1 - \lambda G_0U)^{-1}\big|\hat j_l\big\rangle , \tag{M4.29} T(λ)=−kλ​⟨j^​l​∣∣∣​U(1−λG0​U)−1∣∣∣​j^​l​⟩,(M4.29)

The general recipe has five parts.

  1. Exactly solvable models for each piece: hard sphere, square well, exponential well, Coulomb
    scattering, free motion in coupled channels.
  2. Convergence: in the step size, at the expected order; and in the matching radius, the number of
    partial waves and the number of channels.
  3. Conservation laws and symmetries: unitarity and the optical theorem, ∣S∣≤1|S| \le 1∣S∣≤1 with absorption,
    S=STS = S^TS=ST by time reversal, independence of the coupling order (M3).
  4. Theorems: Levinson’s theorem, Wronskians, threshold laws.
  5. Other codes: the benchmarks against FRESCO and CCFULL in Chapters 6–14.

A code that passes all of these can still be applied to the wrong physics, but it is unlikely to
compute the right physics wrongly.

Summary

  • Work in reduced units, U=V/(ℏ2/2μ)U = V/(\hbar^2/2\mu)U=V/(ℏ2/2μ) with ℏ2/2μ=20.90/μ[u]\hbar^2/2\mu = 20.90/\mu[\text{u}]ℏ2/2μ=20.90/μ[u] MeV fm². The step must
    resolve the local wavelength and the surface; the matching radius must lie beyond the potential.
  • Local error O(hp+1)O(h^{p+1})O(hp+1) means global error O(hp)O(h^p)O(hp). Euler is first order and unstable for waves. RK4 is
    fourth order with slight damping. Numerov is fourth order, needs one potential evaluation per step,
    conserves the amplitude, and has a phase error kr(kh)4/480kr(kh)^4/480kr(kh)4/480.
  • Exponentially decaying tails converge quickly in the matching radius; power-law tails need analytic
    corrections; Coulomb problems must be matched to Coulomb functions.
  • Bound states are found by shooting or by matching outward and inward solutions. Node counting labels
    and brackets them.
  • Coupled equations with closed channels lose linear independence when the closed channel grows faster
    than the coupling decays. Re-orthogonalization by QR, as in FRESCO, restores it at no physical cost.
  • The trapezoidal rule converges exponentially for smooth, decaying or periodic integrands; Gauss–Legendre
    converges exponentially for analytic ones.
  • Split-operator and Crank–Nicolson propagation are unitary and second order in time. Absorbing
    boundaries are imaginary potentials: long enough to avoid reflection, strong enough to stop
    transmission.
  • Divergent Born or iteration series are resummed by Padé approximants, computed from the coefficients
    or by Wynn’s ε algorithm. They converge because the amplitude is meromorphic in the coupling strength.
