M0.1 Why start in one dimension?

Chapter 2 shows that scattering by a central potential in three dimensions reduces to a set of
radial equations, one for each angular momentum. The s-wave radial equation is a one-dimensional
Schrödinger equation on the half-line r≥0r \ge 0r≥0, with a hard wall at the origin. Everything in this
chapter therefore has a three-dimensional counterpart: transmission and reflection become phase
shifts and S-matrix elements, and tunneling governs α decay and sub-barrier fusion. Resonances and
time delays reappear for quasi-bound nuclear states. One dimension lets us see these ideas without
angular-momentum bookkeeping.

Scattering versus bound states. In a bound-state problem we look for the energies at which a
normalizable solution exists. In a scattering problem the energy E>0E > 0E>0 is fixed by the beam, and we
ask what comes out. Consider a potential V(x)V(x)V(x) that approaches constants V∓V_\mpV∓​ as
x→∓∞x \to \mp\inftyx→∓∞, and a particle sent in from the left. The stationary state must then behave as

ψ(x)→{eikx+r e−ikx,x→−∞,t eik′x,x→+∞,k=2m(E−V−)ℏ,k′=2m(E−V+)ℏ.(M0.1)\psi(x) \to \begin{cases} e^{ikx} + r\,e^{-ikx}, & x \to -\infty,\\[2pt] t\,e^{ik'x}, & x \to +\infty, \end{cases} \qquad k = \frac{\sqrt{2m(E - V_-)}}{\hbar},\quad k' = \frac{\sqrt{2m(E - V_+)}}{\hbar}. \tag{M0.1} ψ(x)→{eikx+re−ikx,teik′x,​x→−∞,x→+∞,​k=ℏ2m(E−V−​)​​,k′=ℏ2m(E−V+​)​​.(M0.1)

The incident wave eikxe^{ikx}eikx has unit amplitude. The reflected amplitude rrr and the transmitted
amplitude ttt are what the potential decides. There is no wave coming in from the right. Such a
state is not normalizable; it describes a steady beam (M2 §M2.3 explains how to normalize continuum
states).

(M0.1). On the left,
ψ∗ψ′=ik[1−∣r∣2+r∗e2ikx−r e−2ikx]\psi^*\psi' = ik\left[1 - |r|^2 + r^*e^{2ikx} - r\,e^{-2ikx}\right]ψ∗ψ′=ik[1−∣r∣2+r∗e2ikx−re−2ikx]. The interference terms in the

A useful way to say the same thing: jjj is proportional to the Wronskian ψ∗ψ′−ψ∗′ψ\psi^*\psi' - \psi^{*\prime}\psiψ∗ψ′−ψ∗′ψ,
which is constant because ψ\psiψ and ψ∗\psi^*ψ∗ solve the same equation. The “Wronskian equals flux”

M0.4.2 Opaque barriers and the Gamow factor

When κa≫1\kappa a \gg 1κa≫1, sinh⁡κa≈eκa/2\sinh\kappa a \approx e^{\kappa a}/2sinhκa≈eκa/2 and (M0.5) becomes

(Fig. M0.3(a)). A nucleon 20 MeV below the top of a 3 fm wide barrier has κ≈1\kappa \approx 1κ≈1 fm⁻¹,
and gets through with a probability of order 10−210^{-2}10−2.

M0.4.3 ★ Application: α decay and the Geiger–Nuttall law

In 1928 Gamow, and independently Gurney and Condon, explained α decay as tunneling. An α particle is
assumed to exist inside the parent nucleus and to bounce back and forth with velocity vvv. Each time
it hits the surface, at radius RRR, it tunnels through the Coulomb barrier 2Zde2/r2Z_de^2/r2Zd​e2/r of the daughter
(ZdZ_dZd​ is the daughter’s charge) with probability e−2Ge^{-2G}e−2G. This is an s-wave radial problem, that
is, a one-dimensional problem. For a Coulomb barrier the Gamow integral (M0.9) is elementary:

G=2μQℏ b[arccos⁡x−x(1−x)],b=2Zde2Q,x=Rb,(M0.12)G = \frac{\sqrt{2\mu Q}}{\hbar}\,b\left[\arccos\sqrt{x} - \sqrt{x(1-x)}\right], \qquad b = \frac{2Z_de^2}{Q},\quad x = \frac{R}{b}, \tag{M0.12} G=ℏ2μQ​​b[arccosx​−x(1−x)​],b=Q2Zd​e2​,x=bR​,(M0.12)

where QQQ is the decay energy, μ\muμ the reduced mass and bbb the outer turning point. The decay rate
is the knocking rate times the penetrability, λ=(v/2R) e−2G\lambda = (v/2R)\,e^{-2G}λ=(v/2R)e−2G, and t1/2=ln⁡2/λt_{1/2} = \ln 2/\lambdat1/2​=ln2/λ.
For R≪bR \ll bR≪b the bracket tends to π/2−2x\pi/2 - 2\sqrt{x}π/2−2x​, and the leading term is
2G≈2πZde22μ/Q /ℏ2G \approx 2\pi Z_de^2\sqrt{2\mu/Q}\,/\hbar2G≈2πZd​e22μ/Q​/ℏ. The logarithm of the half-life is therefore nearly
linear in Zd/QZ_d/\sqrt{Q}Zd​/Q​. That is the Geiger–Nuttall law, found empirically in 1911.

M0.5 The square well: transmission resonances

Look again at the well, equation (M0.7). Even an attractive well reflects; slow particles are
reflected almost completely (T∝ET \propto ET∝E as E→0E \to 0E→0). But whenever qa=nπqa = n\piqa=nπ the well becomes perfectly transparent, T=1T = 1T=1. This is the
Ramsauer–Townsend effect, first seen as the near-transparency of noble gases to slow electrons.
The explanation is interference. The wave is reflected partly at each edge of the well; when the
round-trip phase 2qa2qa2qa is a multiple of 2π2\pi2π, the two reflected waves cancel, as in a
Fabry–Pérot interferometer. For the well of Fig. M0.2© (in units ℏ=2m=1\hbar = 2m = 1ℏ=2m=1, V0=1V_0 = 1V0​=1,
a=4a = 4a=4) the resonances lie at E=(nπ/a)2−V0=1.47E = (n\pi/a)^2 - V_0 = 1.47E=(nπ/a)2−V0​=1.47 and 4.554.554.55. In three dimensions the same
effect appears when an s-wave phase shift passes through nπn\pinπ (Chapter 2).

M0.6 The one-dimensional S-matrix

In general waves can arrive from both sides. Call aLa_LaL​ and aRa_RaR​ the amplitudes of the waves coming
in from the left and from the right, and bLb_LbL​ and bRb_RbR​ those of the outgoing waves (assume
V−=V+V_- = V_+V−​=V+​ so all waves have the same kkk). The Schrödinger equation is linear, so

(bLbR)=S(aLaR),S=(rt′tr′),(M0.13)\begin{pmatrix} b_L \\ b_R \end{pmatrix} = S \begin{pmatrix} a_L \\ a_R \end{pmatrix}, \qquad S = \begin{pmatrix} r & t' \\ t & r' \end{pmatrix}, \tag{M0.13} (bL​bR​​)=S(aL​aR​​),S=(rt​t′r′​),(M0.13)

where primed amplitudes refer to incidence from the right. Two symmetries shape SSS.

  • Unitarity, S†S=1S^\dagger S = 1S†S=1, from flux conservation: the total outgoing flux equals the total
    incoming flux for any (aL,aR)(a_L, a_R)(aL​,aR​). This contains ∣r∣2+∣t∣2=1|r|^2 + |t|^2 = 1∣r∣2+∣t∣2=1, ∣r∣=∣r′∣|r| = |r'|∣r∣=∣r′∣ and
    ∣t∣=∣t′∣|t| = |t'|∣t∣=∣t′∣.
  • Symmetry, ST=SS^{\rm T} = SST=S, i.e. t=t′t = t't=t′, from time reversal: for a real potential, ψ∗\psi^*ψ∗ is
    also a solution, with the roles of incoming and outgoing waves exchanged.

scattering decouples into an even and an odd channel. Far away the even solution behaves as

M0.8 Time delay and resonant tunneling

M0.8.1 The Wigner time delay

Write the transmission amplitude as t(k)=∣t∣ eiφ(k)t(k) = |t|\,e^{i\varphi(k)}t(k)=∣t∣eiφ(k), with phases referred to free
propagation, so that t=1t = 1t=1 without a potential. The transmitted packet is
∫ϕ ∣t∣ ei[kx−ωt+φ(k)] dk\int\phi\,|t|\,e^{i[kx - \omega t + \varphi(k)]}\,dk∫ϕ∣t∣ei[kx−ωt+φ(k)]dk. Its centre is where this phase is stationary,
x−vt+dφ/dk=0x - vt + d\varphi/dk = 0x−vt+dφ/dk=0. The packet therefore arrives late by

τW=1vdφdk=ℏ dφdE,(M0.18)\tau_W = \frac{1}{v}\frac{d\varphi}{dk} = \hbar\,\frac{d\varphi}{dE}, \tag{M0.18} τW​=v1​dkdφ​=ℏdEdφ​,(M0.18)

the Wigner time delay. It can be negative: the transmitted packet is then built mainly from the
front of the incident one and seems to arrive early. This reshaping does not violate causality.

M0.8.2 Double barriers and resonant tunneling

Put two barriers around a well. The inner well has quasi-bound states that leak out through the
barriers. When the energy matches one of them, a symmetric double barrier becomes perfectly
transparent, even if each barrier alone transmits only a few percent. This is resonant tunneling,
the principle of the resonant-tunneling diode, and a one-dimensional model of the nuclear
shape resonances of Chapter 2. Near an isolated resonance

T(E)≈(Γ/2)2(E−Er)2+(Γ/2)2,φ rises by π,τW(Er)=2ℏΓ.(M0.19)T(E) \approx \frac{(\Gamma/2)^2}{(E - E_r)^2 + (\Gamma/2)^2}, \qquad \varphi \text{ rises by } \pi, \qquad \tau_W(E_r) = \frac{2\hbar}{\Gamma}. \tag{M0.19} T(E)≈(E−Er​)2+(Γ/2)2(Γ/2)2​,φ rises by π,τW​(Er​)=Γ2ℏ​.(M0.19)

the peak is twice that.

M0.9.2 The split-operator method

To follow a packet in time we apply e−iHΔt/ℏe^{-iH\Delta t/\hbar}e−iHΔt/ℏ repeatedly with H=T+VH = T + VH=T+V. The kinetic
energy is diagonal in momentum space and the potential in position space, so we split

e−iHΔt/ℏ≈e−iVΔt/2ℏ  e−iTΔt/ℏ  e−iVΔt/2ℏ,(M0.22)e^{-iH\Delta t/\hbar} \approx e^{-iV\Delta t/2\hbar}\;e^{-iT\Delta t/\hbar}\;e^{-iV\Delta t/2\hbar}, \tag{M0.22} e−iHΔt/ℏ≈e−iVΔt/2ℏe−iTΔt/ℏe−iVΔt/2ℏ,(M0.22)

with an error of order Δt3\Delta t^3Δt3 per step. The middle factor is applied by a fast Fourier transform
to momentum space and back. Every factor is unitary, so the norm is conserved exactly. The grid is
periodic, so outgoing waves must be removed before they wrap around; a mask that falls smoothly to
zero near the grid ends does this.

Two pitfalls showed up while preparing this chapter.

  1. Compare like with like. On a grid, a barrier occupies whole cells, so a “width 1” barrier on a
    grid of step 0.073 is really 0.95 wide. That alone changes TTT by 2–3 %. Stationary comparisons
    must use the potential exactly as the grid represents it (od.grid_layers), or the edges must be
    placed on cell boundaries, as in this notebook.
  2. The time step. The method is stable for any Δt\Delta tΔt, but sharp edges scatter a little
    probability into the highest momenta the grid can hold, which a large step propagates wrongly.
    When the true transmission is small, these components spoil it. In Fig. M0.8(b), with
    T≈8×10−3T \approx 8\times10^{-3}T≈8×10−3, the error is 20 % at Δt=0.08\Delta t = 0.08Δt=0.08 and below 1.5 % for
    Δt≲dx2/2\Delta t \lesssim dx^2/2Δt≲dx2/2. In other set-ups the error went the other way and reached a factor of
    four. The rule of thumb, in physical units, is Δt≲m dx2/ℏ\Delta t \lesssim m\,dx^2/\hbarΔt≲mdx2/ℏ.

Summary

  • A one-dimensional scattering state has an incident, a reflected and a transmitted wave; the
    constant probability current gives R+T=1R + T = 1R+T=1, with TTT a ratio of fluxes.
  • A step reflects even above its height. Below it reflects totally, with an evanescent tail of
    depth 1/κ1/\kappa1/κ.
  • Barriers are crossed by tunneling, T≈e−2GT \approx e^{-2G}T≈e−2G with the Gamow factor (M0.9). The
    Hill–Wheeler formula describes the region around a smooth barrier top.
  • Gamow’s tunneling model reproduces α-decay half-lives over 24 orders of magnitude, which is the
    Geiger–Nuttall law.
  • Wells and barriers are transparent at interference resonances. The S-matrix is unitary and
    symmetric, and for symmetric potentials T=cos⁡2(δe−δo)T = \cos^2(\delta_e - \delta_o)T=cos2(δe​−δo​). The odd phase shift is
    the s-wave phase shift of Chapter 2.
  • Wave packets split into reflected and transmitted parts with probabilities given by the stationary
    TTT averaged over the packet. The transmitted part is energy-filtered.
  • Resonant tunneling through a double barrier gives T=1T = 1T=1 peaks of width Γ\GammaΓ, a phase jump of
    π\piπ and a Wigner time delay ℏ dφ/dE\hbar\,d\varphi/dEℏdφ/dE of up to 2ℏ/Γ2\hbar/\Gamma2ℏ/Γ.
  • Transfer matrices solve layered potentials exactly. Split-operator propagation is unitary but needs
    Δt≲m dx2/ℏ\Delta t \lesssim m\,dx^2/\hbarΔt≲mdx2/ℏ near sharp edges, and comparisons must use the same discretized
    potential.
